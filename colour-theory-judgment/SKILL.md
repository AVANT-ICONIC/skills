# Colour Theory Judgment

Colour is one of the highest-leverage decisions in a design, and one of the most misunderstood. This skill teaches how to reason about colour so choices transfer to new work, not how to recite colour-meaning charts.

Attribution: passages tagged [creator] distill the argued judgment of Satori Graphics ("The 4 Design Skills That Give You An Unfair Advantage", youtu.be/GEOmxbCs-c8). Untagged passages are the author's synthesis and application guidance built on that teaching.

## 1. Colours have no fixed meanings

[creator] Standard colour theory says red means danger or passion. Yet a luxury watch advert uses red to feel premium, a Formula 1 poster uses red to feel fast, and Netflix uses red as pure brand identity. Same colour, three unrelated feelings. Nobody looks at the watch ad and thinks "danger".

[creator] The missing piece is context. Typography changes a colour's meaning. Imagery changes it. The industry changes it. The audience changes it. The exact same shade of red can be made to feel premium, loud, or playful without ever changing the colour itself. Colour psychology is real but incomplete: treat emotional associations as a guide, never a rule book. Blue can signal trust, but also cold, corporate, clinical, modern, premium, or boring, depending on how it is used.

Bounding note (author): this corrects rigid colour-meaning charts, not the learned conventions design relies on. Functional meanings such as red for errors or green for success are product-culture conventions to keep consistent within a product, not universal rules, and legibility and accessibility requirements for text and UI colour are obligations, not context-dependent preferences.

[creator] Colour also does far less work than people think. Much of what gets credited to colour is actually being carried by hierarchy, contrast, placement, and strategy. Once those are understood, colour becomes much easier to work with.

Application: when a palette feels wrong, do not start by swapping hues. First check whether hierarchy and contrast are doing their jobs; colour is often blamed for a structure problem.

## 2. The five layers of colour strategy

[creator] Think of colour as operating across five layers, in ascending order:

1. **Functional colour** exists so people understand what they are looking at: buttons, navigation, warnings, status indicators, dashboards. Before worrying about emotion or branding, ask: can people understand this? If not, the colour has failed its primary job no matter how good the palette looks.
2. **Emotional colour** is the layer most designers know: warm colours can feel energetic, cool colours calmer, desaturation and tints shift the mood. Useful, but a guide rather than a rule book (see section 1).
3. **Cultural colour** is one of the most overlooked areas. Meanings change dramatically by audience: white reads as weddings and purity across much of the Western world but has historically signalled mourning in parts of Asia; green can communicate sustainability, finance, or religion depending on who is looking. Global audiences make cultural checking non-optional.
4. **Competitive colour**: industries develop colour monopolies - banks lean blue, luxury leans black and gold, eco brands lean green, tech leans blue and purple. The creator argues these associations are reinforced over time, and that this creates a deliberate strategic choice: adopt the industry colour to borrow the associations audiences already hold for that sector, or break from it to avoid disappearing into a sea of identical competitors. Author note: this is the creator's strategic hypothesis, not an established mechanism - borrowing an industry colour does not automatically confer trust, so test the choice against the actual audience and product. Both directions are valid; drifting into either by accident is not.
5. **Strategic colour** is the highest layer. Instead of asking "what colour looks good", ask "what is this colour trying to achieve?" - attract attention, build trust, differentiate. Once that question leads, colour decisions stop being decoration and become far easier.

Application: run the layers in order as an audit. Functional failures (unreadable states, ambiguous buttons) outrank every aesthetic concern; strategic fit outranks personal taste.

## 3. Give every colour a job

[creator] Professional palettes feel cleaner than amateur ones for one fundamental reason: every colour has a clearly defined job. The system:

- **Primary** - the hero colour, most strongly associated with the brand, product, or concept. It carries the identity of the project, and it is where the emotional, cultural, competitive, and strategic decisions surface.
- **Secondary** - supports the primary: adds flexibility, depth, and variation without stealing the spotlight. Can reinforce the emotional direction or add visual character without disrupting the message.
- **Accent** - where attention lives: calls to action, key information, focal points. Functional colour matters most here; accents guide behaviour and build hierarchy, they are not decoration.
- **Neutrals** - the most underrated colours in the palette. They create structure, breathing room, and readability, and they let the primary and accent colours actually do their jobs. Without neutrals, every colour fights for attention and none wins.

Application: if a design feels noisy, count how many colours are competing for attention. The fix is usually more neutral space and one disciplined accent, not a new hue.

## 4. Colour contrast directs attention

[creator] Contrast of colour is the best-known application of contrast. Colours opposite each other on the colour wheel offer the most contrast. But contrast also comes from mixing low-saturated colours with bright bold ones, or pairing grayscale with a single colour (which often reads as a sombre atmosphere). This is why call-to-action buttons and for-sale signs use contrasting colours: contrast draws attention in very easy ways.

[creator] Colour contrast rarely works alone. A Nike poster demonstrates three contrast types at once: colour (white and peach shapes against black), shape (straight angled lines against the smooth curvature of the shoe and logo), and texture (a sandy blurred background against the smooth realistic shoe). All three funnel the eye to the product.

[creator] Colour can also create psychological contrast. A poster covered in skulls - normally read as death and negativity - uses bright, bold, uplifting colours and a playful yellow script, and the result reads as happy. The colour choice contradicts the symbol, and that contradiction is the design's message.

[creator] In UI, an apparel app shows the mechanics plainly: black labels stand out against colour photography and white space; contrasting font colours mark the information the designer wants noticed; bright colour blocks sit on a white-gray background; and the photos themselves carry red-on-blue, blue-on-orange, and turquoise-on-pink pairings. Multiple contrast layers stack to build the reading order.

Application: before adding a colour, decide what it contrasts with and what that contrast is for. An accent that does not contrast its surroundings is not an accent. Check contrast on three axes - hue, saturation, and value/grayscale - because a hue-only distinction that disappears in grayscale is fragile on poor screens and for some viewers.

Author addition (not from the video): opposite colours on a wheel are an aesthetic device, not a legibility guarantee. For text and functional UI colour, measure the actual contrast ratio - as one check among several, since a passing ratio alone is not an accessibility certification. State and actions must not rely on colour alone, and non-text UI components carry their own contrast requirements. A grayscale view is a quick designer's screen for value contrast, not proof of colour-vision accessibility. Primary sources: WCAG 2.2 (https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/), Understanding Use of Color (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color), and Understanding Non-text Contrast (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast).

## 5. A working sequence for new designs

Author synthesis, combining the sections above:

1. **Strategy first.** Write one sentence: what should the colour achieve (attention, trust, differentiation, calm)?
2. **Check the competitive layer.** List the sector's colour monopoly, then choose deliberately to join it or break it.
3. **Check the cultural layer.** Name the actual audiences; flag any colour whose meaning flips for one of them.
4. **Assign jobs.** One primary, support from secondary, one disciplined accent for the action that matters, and generous neutrals.
5. **Build hierarchy with contrast, not colour count.** Use hue, saturation, and value contrast so the eye lands in the intended order; verify the accent still wins in grayscale.
6. **Test function at both ends.** Buttons readable, states unambiguous, type legible on every background: check these constraints when the palette is first assembled, re-check them after every revision, and repeat them as final validation - functional testing is continuous, not a single pass at the end.
7. **Tune emotion** with temperature, saturation, and tints - as adjustment, not foundation.
