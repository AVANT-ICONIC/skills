# Pitch, adaptive competitor research and playable scope

Never confuse marketing novelty, emotional adjectives or a static concept render with proof of the core interaction. Substantial design can benefit from real competitors; small prompts should not become 10-page market studies.

## D05: High concept and elevator pitch writer

Use one line: **player verb + central tradeoff + surprising consequence**. Avoid “X meets Y,” lists of genres or promises that don't happen in minute one. Then check the first physical input and loop actually deliver the promise. For a proposed game with a beautiful setting but vague verbs, start with controls and choices before rewriting lore.

**Test:** A reader can repeat the hook and name what they'd actually press; the pitch isn't just plausible copywriting.

## D06: Game market analyst

First state independent hypotheses about mechanic, target player, session length, platform and motivation. If current research tools exist and the task is substantial, compare actual *mechanical decisions* in known alternatives: repeated verbs, failure pressure, upgrade structure, cooperative roles, accessibility and price/market assumptions. Seek official gameplay material and player/community tests where possible. Cite actual sources for external facts and label observed data versus inference. Do not invent store metrics, launch dates, popularity or direct play experience.

**Differentiation matrix:** For each genuine comparator, what player repeatedly chooses there, what they choose here, and why the new tradeoff changes strategy. Different graphics alone are not a mechanic. If research is blocked, retain hypotheses and mark the consequential gap, rather than holding the brainstorming hostage to a web browser.

## D20: Scope and feature prioritizer

Maintain a **vertical slice boundary**: one controllable verb, two viable choices, state consequence, repeat, a small failure/recovery and a new pressure. Mark essential, experiment, stretch and cut features. Network multiplayer, procedural generation, voice acting and elaborate skill trees are not automatically essential because the idea sounds ambitious. For high-vision requests do not preemptively discard bold directions; show the smallest cheap trial to challenge the interesting part.

**Tradeoff test:** If removing a feature leaves the core fun hypothesis unchanged, defer it. If a giant animation is necessary only to impress reviewers, preserve it as later polish instead of replacing real choice with spectacle.

## Adaptive uncertainty rules

Differentiate **source fact**, **implementation observed**, **logical consequence**, **audience hypothesis**, **human behavior observed**, and **untested fun conjecture**. A designer may predict engagement, not certify it. When citing competitor mechanics from memory without fresh verification, don't present this as a live research finding.
