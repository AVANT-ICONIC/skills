# Logo Design Judgment

A logo is a communication device, not decoration. Its primary job is to identify a brand and be memorable - not to be pretty, advertise, or show off creativity. And it does not have to show what the business does [creator]: Apple sells no apples. A logo is an emotion and a memory hook aimed at a specific audience. Every decision traces back to research about the brand, its audience, and where the mark will live. If a choice cannot be explained by that research, it is a guess.

Core sequence: **research -> concept -> shape -> test -> refine -> system -> deliver.** Do not skip to shape. Do not stop before test.

Attribution note: this skill is a synthesis of one creator's demonstrated workflow (Satori Graphics), distilled by the skill author from the public videos listed in references/source-notes.md. Items marked [creator] are his argued judgment, shown with examples in the videos but not independently proven; items marked [common] are broadly held in the design field. The tags mark the primary lessons, not every line. Treat both as strong defaults to apply with brief-specific reasoning, not laws.

## 1. Research before any sketching

Skipping research is the single biggest handicap in logo work [creator, demonstrated in his timed experiments: the no-research results stayed generic; his normal process allows 1-2 weeks of research and ideation before designing].

Answer these in writing before drawing anything:

- What does the brand sell, and what makes it different from alternatives?
- Who is the audience (not the client - the people the brand must reach)? What do they value?
- What should the brand make people feel? Name 3-5 adjectives. Every concept you present should have a stated emotional response it aims for in that audience [creator].
- What are the brand's core values? Even a one-day exercise starts there [creator, Zara exercise: creativity, adaptability, leadership, quality drove both redesigns].
- How does the brand speak? Write five words the brand would *never* say, five it overuses, and one sentence it would post to introduce itself. Voice sets the emotional temperature; the mark must match it.
- Where will the logo actually live (app icon, avatar circle, clothing label, storefront, print, motion)?

Working methods from the source material:

- **Keyword pairs** [creator]: pull keywords from the client's own message, check they resonate with the *audience* (not just the owner), expand each into related words, then pair words. Keep at least three pairs covering target audience, brand mission, and industry. Pairs can generate names and marks ("iron + grip" -> "Iron Grip" -> grip -> fist -> simplified geometry). Worked examples [creator]: Innocent smoothies = a halo ("innocent") plus a hand-drawn, less-corporate style ("genuine"); Apple's mission yields "innovative experience".
- **Mood board** [creator]: collect niche references and annotate them (e.g. "bold sans-serif + geometry throughout this niche; serif too formal for martial arts"). The board decides what to conform to and what to avoid.
- **Learn the niche's lingo** [creator]: terminology and insider language surface concepts outsiders miss.
- **Profile the audience, not the client** [creator technique, not verified research method]: for a quick audience signal, scan the social-media bios of a successful competitor's followers (age, profession, interests). Build a psychographic profile: personality, values, hobbies, lifestyle, goals, pain points. He uses AI to draft such a profile but deliberately applies it to the design himself.
- **Client questionnaire early** [creator]: a short one - what makes the business different, who it is for, what it offers. It anchors research before any sketching.
- **One-sentence filter** [creator]: condense brief + research into one sentence (client need + aesthetic direction). Every later design choice must answer to it.

Turn vague brief words into judgeable requirements. "Modern, bold, premium" mean nothing until converted: "must read on moving equipment", "confident but not aggressive", "distinct from these three named competitors." Requirements let you compare concepts honestly.

## 2. Concept: start past the obvious

The amateur move is grabbing the first industry association: coffee brand = coffee bean, outdoor brand = mountain, innovation = lightbulb. Those ideas are not automatically wrong, but starting where everyone starts forces you to fight for distinctiveness.

Generate three different starting points instead:

1. **What** the business does (the literal subject).
2. **How** it does it (its method, speed, care, attitude).
3. **What people get** from it (the outcome or feeling).

A city cycling brand: *what* gives wheels and cogs; *how* gives slipping through tight spaces efficiently; *what people get* gives independence and escape. The second and third rows produce marks the first never could. [creator]: a literal bike for a mountain-bike brand blends into obscurity; Silverback uses no mountain and no bike - an iconic animal mark plus a slanted logotype for motion - and stays memorable.

More concept guidance:

- **Overused starting points** [creator, as cautionary examples, not categorical bans]: tree-people, windows in real-estate/construction marks, overlaid double letters with no brand meaning, the lightbulb for ideas, the arc swoosh through lettering, colored dots slapped on (they also fail at small sizes), and reflections-with-gradients (dated look). These come from cheap templates and mass AI generation. Any of them can work with genuine creative flair and a brand reason; the failure mode is using them as defaults.
- **Cliche first ideas are fine as raw material** [creator]: the fist was the obvious first thought for "Iron Grip"; the skill is simplifying it to geometry, not avoiding the thought.
- **A first draft is a springboard, not a commitment** [creator]: given a collaborator's or AI's draft, keep the kernel (a good logotype) and replace what is literal or generic (a cliche speech-bubble mark) with an abstract, iconic interpretation. A decent external concept plus your refinement can beat starting from zero - time is the biggest asset.
- **Look for negative space tied to a keyword** [creator, Bolt/Koto case]: the lightning bolt sits as negative space between the L and T of a bold wordmark. A wordmark with an idea inside it beats a wordmark with an icon bolted on. Keep cutout shapes compound/editable in the vector file.
- **One-sentence rule**: if you cannot explain the concept in one sentence, it is too complicated. Simplify until you can.
- **Sketch first, software second**: bring sketches into the vector tool as locked guides. Opening the shape tools without a concept produces decoration.
- **Iterate by copying forward** [creator]: duplicate the design and change one thing per pass. The first copy-forward pass usually beats the original; treat that as a working habit, not a guarantee. Small moves can have outsized effects (a tiny scale-down plus slight rotation fixed a whole composition).
- **Know when to stop** [creator]: more time does not mean more design. When a concept has run its course, stop - his 10-minute result arguably beat his 1-hour result in the same brief. Treat this as one demonstrated example, not a universal law about time budgets.

## 3. Shape, type and color decisions

**Shape language** [creator's rationale vocabulary with examples, not validated psychology - match to the audience and culture you are designing for]:
- Circles/ovals: community, togetherness (Mastercard).
- Squares/rectangles and straight edges: strength, reliability, stability, balance.
- Triangles: dynamic, power, stability (context-dependent).
- Angled/slanted lines: motion, forward drive (Adidas).
- Man-made subjects favor straight, geometric construction; natural or living subjects favor curves and organic grids [creator] (circular grid behind a kangaroo mark).

**Category choice** (pick deliberately, not by habit):
- Lettermark/monogram: long or unwieldy names (NASA). Hard to make iconic.
- Wordmark: new brands that need their name seen (Google, FedEx, Samsung). Keep it short.
- Brandmark/pictorial: universal, language-free, deep bond when earned (Apple, Target). Hardest to make unique.
- Combination mark: most common and flexible; package icon + wordmark + combined versions.
- Emblem: authority, tradition, teams/institutions (Starbucks). Can feel dated.
- Mascot: friendly, food/family brands. High risk, hard to age well.
- Do not add a symbol because "a logo is supposed to have one." Some brands are better served by a wordmark alone. A logo does not have to literally show what the company does [common; creator's quiz doctrine].

**Typeface as message** [creator's ladder, a rubric rather than a ranking]:
- Amateur: a generic or favorite font mismatched to the brief (Papyrus for a rugged brand), auto tracking, no manual kerning.
- Pro: typeface psychology matched to the brief, manual kerning by eye (outline the text or nudge by keyboard), optical spacing.
- Master: a bespoke or customized logotype, with the symbol and type sharing the same psychological register for the audience.
- Useful signals [creator]: lowercase reads approachable/friendly; italics read dynamic/forward-moving; bold condensed italic sans reads as strength and motion (Nike); a light refined serif reads as formal elegance (Aston Martin).
- Check: does the typeface match the brand voice, stay legible from header to favicon, and survive weight/spacing adjustments?

**Color**:
- Driven by brand identity and the emotional effect on the audience - not client preference, not trends [creator, common].
- **Conform vs contrast is a deliberate decision** [creator]: Taco Bell's purple stands apart from fast-food red/yellow. Say which you are doing and why. Origin can also choose the palette (IKEA uses the Swedish flag); emotion can split it (FedEx pairs action-orange with a calming, professional purple).
- **The mark must work without its color effects**: test flat and grayscale. If recognition collapses without the gradient, the form is weak [creator; amateur tier relies on the gradient for the logo to "work"].
- **Know when to break convention** [creator's master tier]: a desaturated soft red for an insurance brand aimed at a radical, elite audience - warming, bold, differentiating - where the pro-safe choice was trust-blue. Rule-breaking is a skill applied to a researched audience, not a license.
- Test inverted/dark-mode variants deliberately.

## 4. Craft rules that separate pro from amateur

- Minimalism removes excess, *not* identity. If the mark could belong to five brands, you cut too much.
- No effect crutches: gradients, bevels, drop shadows and filters on a weak form read as clip art. Form, psychology and composition do the talking.
- Uniform micro-whitespace: define a unit (1X) and hold it across the whole mark.
- Consistent angles: shared angle values across elements create invisible order.
- Grids serve best as finishing tools more than universal starting points [creator's rule of thumb]: circular grids for organic marks, straight/45-degree grids for geometric ones. A construction grid can still scaffold a complex geometric mark, and grid-snap builds clean shapes - but judge the result optically.
- **Optical beats mathematical alignment** [creator, demonstrated]: a logotype flush to the grid can still read unbalanced because letterforms carry different visual weight (a hard straight edge next to a letter with open space). Nudge until the eye agrees. Practical corollary: Illustrator's Align uses the text bounding box, not the glyph edges - outlining the text changes the alignment result. Use tools plus eye; grids for shapes, judgment for type.
- **Stroke-built marks** [creator mechanics]: start with a crude pen stroke, do not neaten early; round caps and corners for smoothness; width tool for a subtle taper; smooth tool; gradient *along* the stroke if color is wanted; negative space that gradually widens to direct the eye; obsess over anchor points only at the end.
- Anchor point discipline: before calling a mark finished, inspect the skeleton in outline view. Delete redundant points; no stray points on straight segments; each curve does one job. Extra points create tiny tension shifts that make a logo feel "off" without anyone knowing why, and they scale worse.
- **Non-destructive exploration** [creator]: instead of Pathfinder Minus Front (which merges), fill the front shape with the background color to preview cutouts and keep rearranging; or after Minus Front, use Direct Selection + Shift to still move inner shapes.
- **Proof the file elsewhere** [creator]: export PNG/SVG and view it in another app or screen (he opens the SVG in a browser). Alignment and balance problems invisible in the design tool show up elsewhere.
- Save a custom swatch group per client for cross-session consistency [creator].
- **Design in black and white first** [creator]: decide the form without color bias; color ideas from research wait until the shape works.
- Execution quality: ship an editable vector master (vectors scale to any size; raster exports are valid delivery formats derived from the master, not substitutes for it), even spacing, consistent stroke weights (unless intentionally organic), clean exports, sane file names.
- Never use stock or template graphics in a logo [creator caution: originality plus legal exposure; the legal claim is his warning, not verified law].

## 5. Test every candidate

Run these on any mark before presenting or shipping:

1. **Small-size test**: shrink to favicon size. If the full wordmark falls apart at that size, do not force it - use or design a simplified small-size symbol instead. Full wordmark, icon, and stripped symbol versions are a system, not indecision (Instagram; Airbnb's wordmark vs symbol favicon; the Premier League's four formats). A responsive logo adapts to sizes and platforms.
2. **Blur test**: apply a blur filter. Still recognizable -> strong silhouette (a pixelated Apple mark passes; a detail-heavy monogram falls apart).
3. **3-second recall** [creator]: show for three seconds, hide, ask the person to describe or draw it from memory. Add-on: collect the adjectives they use and check them against the brief.
4. **Lineup test**: show briefly, then ask them to find it in a lineup of similar marks. If several people pick the same wrong logo, compare those two shapes - you found a real problem.
5. **Unexplained adjective test**: show the mark with no explanation and ask how it makes them feel. The adjectives should match the brief.
6. **Niche-mix test** [creator]: place the final concept among 4-5 existing logos from the niche at the same size. This exposes unintentional similarity and brand confusion before the market does.
7. **Ownability gap**: put 20 logos from the client's industry on one artboard, names covered. How many could swap places unnoticed? If your eye does not land on yours, you designed for comfort. Claim space with proportion, tension, graphic voice, or a simplification others have not dared. Ownable, not weird.
8. **Context test**: mock the mark into its real habitat early - avatar circle at actual size, website header, clothing label, storefront, bag. Test the *smallest* important placement first; it reveals problems a big artboard hides. Compare competing concepts in identical conditions: plain black first, then the real environment. Judge packaging in context too: a mark can be weaker standalone yet right on the product (7Up's redesign works on the can).
9. **Brand-name removal**: remove the name. Would people still recognize and remember the mark? (Purely typographic logos are the allowed exception.)
10. **Busy-to-iconic check** [creator]: an over-detailed emblem reads as a digital illustration, not a logo (a cluttered HOME REPAIR badge vs its simplified house-arrow rewrite - the simple mark is absorbed in milliseconds and can stand alone without the wordmark). If the mark needs its detail to survive, simplify until it does not.
11. **Second opinion / fresh eyes** [creator]: after 50 hours you cannot see your own design objectively. Get an outside viewer; if nobody is available, shelve it for a day or two and re-inspect.

## 6. Motion and habitat

Logos live in feeds, dark mode, and motion, not on white artboards.

- Decide the mark's behavior in at least one real application while the concept is still fluid; a strong mark's form suggests its own animation and layout behavior (a sweeping tapered stroke becomes a wipe reveal).
- Motion follows the mark's existing direction and the brand's character: a refined mark gets a slow reveal and a hold; the same mark with a fast bounce turns playful. If the movement contradicts the brief, the movement is wrong.
- Animation is the next media step for identity, not a fad [creator opinion]: respect instant recognition and brand essence; same for sonic logos.
- Check dark-mode and inverted variants deliberately. Brands increasingly expect the mark to work with motion graphics from day one [creator] - ask at concept time whether it will translate.
- The logo is not the brand [creator]: it is one cog in the identity machine and must work in harmony with the wider identity; a mark that fights the identity confuses the market.

## 7. Decide between concepts, and present

- Compare in identical, correct conditions: same background, same size, plain black first, then the real placement. Judge against the written requirements, not taste. A beautifully drawn mark that communicates the wrong character fails.
- Judge redesigns and trends in application context, and avoid pile-ons [creator]: polarizing redesigns (Nokia 2023) deserve analysis, not fanboy outrage. Functionality and memorability over surface aesthetics.
- **Pitch by solving the brief** [creator]: the best presentation demonstrates how the design solves the brief's stated problems - not a parade of three concepts, not a mockup flood. Small, deliberate details become talking points.
- **Pre-empt objections** [creator]: anticipate the client's pushback and prepare the weaker variant on purpose (e.g. a same-font version) to demonstrate why your choice wins.
- **The client always wins** [creator]: when the client picks the option you like least, that is their right. Present your reasoning, then deliver their choice professionally.
- **Presentation craft** [creator]: open by restating what makes a good logo (3-5 attributes - it limits purely subjective feedback) and the problem the client hired you to solve; a brand-color split screen (mark in white on brand color, and inverted) lands well as the first visual; use mockups of human, specific situations (a pen, a badge, real people from the target audience interacting with the mark); remind the room the logo is for the audience, not for the client. Selling the idea is as important as the design itself.

## 8. Trends: a reason vs a bandwagon

[creator's argued position, from his trend-critique video]

- A valid reason for a design choice and jumping on a trend are different things. Early flat simplification was justified (screens, scalability); much trend-chasing 3D is not. The rookie move is reading a trends article and jamming it into the next brief without asking if it fits.
- His per-trend tests: does it risk dating the identity (nostalgia styling)? Does it limit adaptability (hand-rendered heritage styles)? Is it cookie-cutter (mass AI marks)? Does it contradict another proclaimed trend in the same list (monogram-everything vs heritage)? Would the client need to rebrand when the cycle turns?
- Bottom line: brief + audience + craft is the durable foundation. He almost never considers trends when designing. Label: this is one respected practitioner's stance, argued from first principles - useful as a challenge to trend-chasing, not as proof that trends never matter.

## 9. Handoff checklist

- Every version has a purpose and a named home: full logo, icon, monochrome, inverse, stacked and horizontal lockups. Do not create variants to fill a brand guide - but do ship the favicon and alternate layouts a real identity needs [creator's master tier: the identity works on every platform while staying related].
- Deliver color modes for the real world: RGB for screen, CMYK for print, Pantone for consistent spot color [creator].
- The mark must survive with gradient and without; deliver flat and grayscale versions.
- Files named so a stranger knows which to upload where; show each version in use; flag anything that needs a physical sample before approval (print, embroidery).
- Include vector masters, correct color modes, and the spacing/1X unit documentation so future designers can extend the system.
- Process note for client work [creator]: contract before designing; discovery covers business, goals, pain points, audience.
